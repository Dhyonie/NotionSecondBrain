# Interview — phase 1

Raw findings. Everything in the spec traces back to something here.

## 1. What falls through the cracks

All five categories: commitments to other people, ideas, dated admin, where he left off, and
research fragments that never connect. Plus, in his words: *"interests, projects I want to
start or have started, planning them, making a gameplan and setting them in the calendar —
everything becomes too overwhelming. Finances, email replies, tasks or ideas."*

**Worst one: dated admin.** Appointments, renewals, bills, deadlines.

**Key finding.** The problem isn't only losing things. When he gathers everything up, the pile
itself becomes overwhelming and he backs away. That's a different failure from forgetting, and
it means a system that captures everything and then *shows* everything would recreate the
overwhelm in a tidier font.

## 2. How thoughts reach him

All four moments — driving, mid-task, in bed, during conversation. In his words: *"they always
hit me at a moment when I'm doing something else and rarely when I need to remember."*

Realistic capture: **typing quickly into his phone**. Voice isn't required first.

**Key finding.** This is context-dependent memory — the cue that fires the thought isn't
present later when he could act on it. So capture and reminding must be fully separated, and
reminding must come *to* him, because the moment he'd think to check is the moment his brain
isn't cueing it.

## 3. What he's tried and abandoned

Everything: paper, notes apps, to-do apps, full workspaces. All four death modes fired —
stopped putting things in, put things in but never looked again, got messy and couldn't face
it, and setting it up was the fun part.

His own additions: *"either a steep learning curve or a system I have to build with 0 knowledge
on how to do it"* and *"a system that I need to keep maintaining and sometimes not so efficient
to use."*

**Key finding — the most important in the interview.** Every system handed him two jobs on top
of the actual work: design it yourself with no prior knowledge, then maintain it forever. He was
asked to be the architect and the janitor of a building he only wanted to live in. The tool was
never the problem. This is why maintenance must land entirely on Claude — if it doesn't, this
attempt fails identically.

## 4. What he wants to see

All four — what to do now, hard deadlines, what he was in the middle of, what he owes people.
Plus: *"depending on later decisions I want to know the overview and what is on that day to do
in a certain order."*

Delivery: **once a day, morning.**

**Key finding.** An ordered sequence isn't a list. A list asks him to choose, and choosing under
a big pile is where he backs away. A sequence has the choosing already done.

## 5. Projects and areas

**Projects named:** the automation app, learning Figma / design work, order-flow trading,
business systems for starter businesses, his entrepreneurship business, acquiring new skills,
acquiring a new car, and a "full marketing system" (social ads, content, webpage, CRM,
clickfunnels, branding, email campaigns).

**Areas:** finances and admin, home and personal life, learning and research. He did *not* tick
email as an area despite naming email replies in Q1 — later confirmed email belongs in the system.

**Key finding.** Several of these aren't projects, they're ambitions with no finish line, and the
marketing system is a *program* — seven projects wearing one name. Mixed into one list, the
ambitions make the whole thing feel infinite.

## 6. Control boundaries

Claude may freely: file and sort anything captured. Everything else waits — anything another
person sees, deleting or archiving, structural changes, marking a project active. His phrasing:
*"anything that pertains a system change or irreversible activity."*

**Key finding.** This generalises as one-way versus two-way doors. It only appears to conflict
with "maintenance must land on Claude" if approving and doing are the same act. They aren't:
he keeps the decisions, Claude keeps the labour.

## 7. What would make him quit

The brief becoming noise, maintenance returning, capture getting slow — and his own addition:
*"not understanding the assignment or guessing the wrong thing."*

Daily time available: **5–15 minutes.**

**Key finding.** The last one is a trust failure rather than an effort failure, and it compounds
fastest — after two or three bad guesses he'd start double-checking everything, which is the
maintenance problem in disguise. Countermeasure: every filed item carries a visible line stating
the assumption, so a wrong guess costs five seconds to correct.
